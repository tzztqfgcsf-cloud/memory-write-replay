\begin{abstract}
Memory-write evaluation must distinguish completing required changes from introducing incorrect writes. We diagnose these outcomes through specification-based replay: holding saved extractions and proposals fixed, varying admission rules, and scoring executed states. The internally prespecified complete-pipeline comparison did not establish a completion advantage over general review. On 48 authored Korean cases, 21 model configurations, and three repetitions (3,024 episodes), replay yields three findings. First, evidence-checking rules recover 11 corrections on Gemini 2.5 Flash, but releasing every eligible held correction recovers the same 11. The holds originate in empty extracted fact lists; revising extraction lets the unchanged gate complete five of five selected corrections, against one of five under the original instruction. Second, 12 of 21 configurations have zero exposure to decisions distinguishing the correction rules; only 49 controlled-path outputs from 12 cases reach that boundary. Third, mechanically accepting candidate tuple variants reduces the gate's avoided reference violations from 82 to 30 and increases lost completed changes from 40 to 92 on saved general-review proposals. These matches lack independent human validation. On real DSTC2 recognition hypotheses, replay instead locates holds in disagreement or incomplete support across hypotheses. The contribution is a diagnosis of failure attribution, decision opportunity, and reference sensitivity within the tested pipeline; generalization to independent memory writers remains untested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Conversational assistants increasingly maintain persistent memories of their users \cite{cite10}. Updating these memories requires completing requested changes while preserving facts that should remain unchanged. Systems propose additions, revisions, and deletions \cite{cite4,cite6,cite21,cite39,cite40}, but downstream answers or final-state scores \cite{cite20,cite37} do not identify which stage produced an error. A correction may be proposed correctly yet withheld because an earlier extraction omitted its supporting fact.

We study this problem by separating generation, admission, and execution. Generation produces extracted facts and proposed operations; admission determines which operations may execute; execution changes the stored state. Our specification-based replay holds the generated artifacts fixed, applies alternative admission rules, and checks the whole resulting state against an explicit reference. The four rules range from keeping corrections without shared extracted support to releasing eligible held corrections or requiring literal or target-linked evidence. Their \emph{exposure} is the set of outputs on which they can decide differently, determined from gate logs before scoring.

The internally prespecified complete-pipeline comparison did not establish a completion advantage over two-call general review (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). The replay analyses were developed afterward to explain the observed outcomes. Here, ``prespecified'' refers to the original internal protocol, not public preregistration.

The study uses 48 authored Korean cases, 21 model configurations, and three repetitions per case, yielding 3,024 episodes on 48 distinct cases. Its three main findings are (Figure~\ref{fig:1}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Extraction omissions can create recoverable holds.} On Gemini 2.5 Flash, all three rules that relax the agreement gate recover the same 11 corrections, so recovery alone does not establish a benefit from evidence-based selection. In a follow-up on five selected cases, revising extraction lets the unchanged gate complete five corrections, against one under the original instruction; transfer to new cases remains untested (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}).
\item \emph{Many ties arise without a decision that could separate the rules.} Twelve of 21 configurations have zero exposure on controlled-path proposals. Across the panel, only 49 outputs from 12 cases reach the decision boundary. Exposure therefore determines which ties test competing admission decisions (Section~\ref{sec:exposure-results}).
\item \emph{Reference conventions change the apparent benefit of gating.} Mechanically accepting candidate variants of required facts reduces avoided reference violations from 82 to 30 and increases lost completed changes from 40 to 92 on general-review proposals. Available pairwise correction-rule differences are unchanged under the two references, but missing replays limit this conclusion (Section~\ref{sec:reference-results}).
\end{itemize}

These findings concern attribution, opportunity, and specification, respectively. For fixed generated artifacts and admission rules, exposure is determined from gate decisions before reference-based scoring; restricted to each endpoint population, it bounds the rule differences that the comparison can reveal. Table~\ref{tab:1} places this evaluation alongside eleven memory-write studies. The remaining analyses examine target-linked evidence, decompose the complete-pipeline comparison, and apply replay to a learned verifier and real recognition hypotheses (Sections~\ref{sec:target} and \ref{sec:pipeline}--\ref{sec:dstc2}). The findings establish mechanisms in the tested pipeline, not their prevalence across systems.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Memory Updates and Admission in LLM Agents}
Several systems give LLM agents a persistent memory that is updated through explicit operations. MemGPT treats the context window as fast memory and moves information to and from external storage through function calls \cite{cite21}. Mem0 extracts candidate facts and applies one of four operations to existing memories: add, update, delete, or no operation \cite{cite6}. A-Mem stores memories as linked notes whose attributes are revised as new notes arrive \cite{cite39}, and MemoryBank, recursive summarization, SeCom, LD-Agent, and Generative Agents maintain or select conversational history in related ways \cite{cite15,cite22,cite23,cite35,cite47}. Closest to the correction decision studied here, Bae et al.\ cast memory management in Korean long-term conversation as pairwise operations between a stored sentence and a new session summary (pass, replace, append, or delete) \cite{cite4}. They compared trained classifiers with natural language inference models mapped to these operations and evaluated operation accuracy, set-level overlap, and human ratings of later sessions. Memory-R1 learns add, update, delete, and no-operation decisions from a reward based on downstream answer correctness \cite{cite40}, and Zep invalidates, rather than deletes, knowledge-graph edges that newer information contradicts \cite{cite26}.

A recent line of work treats the write itself as an admission decision. A-MAC learns an admission policy from interpretable factors (future utility, factual confidence, semantic novelty, temporal recency, and a content-type prior) and evaluates admission decisions against labeled candidate memories \cite{cite48}; ConsistencyGate samples the same model several times to score whether a candidate fact is supported by its source and admits the fact above a threshold \cite{cite49}; and DerivAudit audits whether a stored memory follows from the history available when it was written, finding that support often lies beyond the writer's citations and that verifiers still admit many unsupported memories \cite{cite51}. STALE evaluates whether agents notice that a stored memory has been implicitly invalidated by later information \cite{cite50}. Memory writes are also an attack surface: AgentPoison and MINJA show that poisoned or injected records, the latter inserted through ordinary queries, later steer agent behavior \cite{cite5,cite7}. In our setting, the surrounding history is supplied rather than retrieved, so retrieval failure is not confounded with admission failure; how the position of information affects its use is a separate question \cite{cite16}.

\subsection{State Revision and Competing Hypotheses}
Dialogue-state tracking already models state revision. SOM-DST predicts an operation for each slot before generating values \cite{cite14}, and its oracle analysis attributes most errors to the operation predictor rather than to value generation, an early stage-level diagnosis of the kind we apply to memory writes. AG-DST amends an initially generated state \cite{cite33}, dataflow synthesis supports executable dialogue programs and non-destructive revision \cite{cite3}, and PoA generates semantic-action code that updates the dialogue state \cite{cite31}. Other trackers select the relevant turns, suppress incorrect values, or rewrite queries to resolve coreference \cite{cite11,cite17,cite34}. The second Dialog State Tracking Challenge supplied competing speech-recognition hypotheses for every turn and scored the tracked state turn by turn \cite{cite12}; its setting is close to ours, although there the tracker produces the state itself, and Section~\ref{sec:dstc2} replays our admission rules on its hypotheses. MultiWOZ~2.2 removes values that the system offered before the user accepted them \cite{cite44}, and MultiWOZ~2.4 removes values that only the system mentioned \cite{cite42}; both revisions show why the timing and source of evidence matter. MultiWOZ~2.2 also lists the surface variants of a value as equally correct \cite{cite44}, motivating a sensitivity analysis of matching conventions (Section~\ref{sec:reference-results}); this does not validate our mechanical subject- and value-containment rules. Our comparison intervenes after extraction and proposal are fixed: it evaluates the permission to execute a proposal and thus complements these state-generation methods.

\subsection{Evidence, Clarification, and Evaluation}
Clarification has long been modeled as a decision under uncertainty \cite{cite43,cite24,cite8,cite9,cite45}, although human clarification is only weakly tied to annotated ambiguity \cite{cite19} and is not mirrored by model uncertainty \cite{cite32}; an uncertain detail can be unnecessary for the next action yet consequential for a durable write. Disagreeing readings resemble the inter-context conflicts studied between retrieved sources \cite{cite38}, and a quoted span shows that a value occurs in the source, not that it supports the proposed fact \cite{cite1}; this distinction motivates comparing literal evidence with target-linked witnesses. Attribution to identified sources can be defined and rated by people \cite{cite25}, entailment-based checkers detect factual inconsistency \cite{cite13}, LLM judges can approximate human preferences \cite{cite46}, and agreement across readings resembles self-consistency over sampled outputs \cite{cite36}. $\tau$-bench scores agents by the final database state after tool use \cite{cite41}, the kind of executed-state endpoint that we apply to a single turn. Self-Refine and Reflexion revise outputs at inference time \cite{cite18,cite29}; we use a two-call general review as the alternative complete pipeline, and because the choice of instruction can itself change results \cite{cite2}, every condition receives the same task facts and retention constraints. Automatic and human assessments can agree \cite{cite30} or rank systems differently \cite{cite28}, so agreement has to be established for each endpoint. Table~\ref{tab:1} audits what memory-write evaluations report.

Within the eleven studies in this reporting audit, A-MAC and DerivAudit combine fixed candidates with both error types; the audited reports do not provide the exposure quantity defined here. This is a finding about the selected reports, not an exhaustive claim about memory-write research. Our evaluation adds exposure and reference sensitivity to whole-state comparisons of edits to identified records under competing readings. We do not directly benchmark A-MAC, ConsistencyGate, or DerivAudit because their candidate-label tasks differ from the executed-state task here. Section~\ref{sec:verifier} instead applies a learned verifier to the same saved proposals.

Re-evaluations of inference benchmarks, recommenders, and metric learning show how evaluation choices can change conclusions without a new model \cite{cite54,cite55,cite56}. Counterfactual replay \cite{cite57} and performance bounds under incomplete labels \cite{cite58} provide related methodological context. Here, exposure complements power analysis \cite{cite52} by bounding the endpoint difference possible on the saved outputs.

\section{Task and Decision Boundary}\label{sec:task}
A test case is $x=(h,T,M,\pi)$, consisting of the preceding context $h$; an indexed sequence $T=(t_1,\ldots,t_k)$ of alternative readings of one current utterance, which may coincide; an initial memory state $M$; and a case-level retention permission $\pi$. The readings are simultaneous alternatives for the same speaker's turn, not separate speakers or successive turns. A record has a persistent identifier and a fact tuple (subject, relation, value, time). The model proposes an action $a$, a reply $u$, and an ordered list of memory operations $Q$; the application executes the admitted operations in one transaction and returns the state $M'$ together with an execution log $\rho$.

Four operations are available: APPEND, CORRECT, HOLD, and no-write. A correction targets an existing record and replaces its value while keeping its identifier, whereas a hold leaves a proposed fact uncommitted. Retention permission is separate from factual support, because a clearly stated fact may still be disallowed for storage. Supplying the initial state lets the evaluation distinguish a new fact from an obsolete or protected one without having to infer it. For each case, the reference specifies the allowed actions, the required and forbidden tuples, the records that must remain unchanged, the obsolete versions to retire, and any records that must no longer appear; all of these requirements are checked on the executed state.

We assess required change and preservation together. On a positive case, at least one required fact is absent or an obsolete version must be retired, so holding every proposal fails. A correct target update can still fail whole-state conformity when it also appends a fact outside the requested edit. We score the action and persistent state against the reference and inspect reply--state discrepancies in selected traces; reply truthfulness is not a scored endpoint. In tables, ``Viol.'' abbreviates reference violations. Table~\ref{tab:2} defines the terms and endpoints used throughout. A \emph{case} is a distinct input specification and reference; an \emph{episode} is one configuration's run on one case in one repetition. Replaying one saved proposal under four rules creates four evaluated conditions, not four independent episodes.

\section{Correction Admission Rules}\label{sec:rules}
\subsection{One Proposal and Four Admission Decisions}\label{sec:admission}
The controlled path makes two model calls. The first returns an extraction $F$: for each reading, the atomic facts that the reading supports, and, for each pair of a stored record and a reading, one correction witness with a polarity label (affirmed, negated, uncertain, or absent), a new value, and a verbatim quotation. The second call receives the test case together with $F$, marked as fallible, and generates the final proposal. Every admission rule receives the identical test case, extraction, and proposal, together with an isolated copy of $M$, so changing the rule requires no further model call (Figure~\ref{fig:2}).

\agree{} applies to every proposed APPEND and CORRECT operation. It admits an operation when the exact subject--relation--value--time tuple is supported by the fact list of every reading or is already present in $M$; otherwise, it changes the operation to HOLD. \agree{} was the conservative gate of the original protocol, fixed before collection, and is meant to keep a fact out of memory when the readings disagree about it. Shared support requires that the extraction validly cover all readings. Because the test compares tuples exactly as extracted, and because the extraction schema allows a reading's fact list to be empty, a correction can appear in the proposal and in the witnesses yet be missing from the intersection of the extracted fact lists. This is how the held set arises.

Let $H(x,F,Q)$ be the set of operation indices $j$ for which \agree{} holds a proposed CORRECT only because its support is not shared, although every reading is validly covered. All differences between \agree{} and the other three rules lie within $H$; outside $H$, every rule keeps \agree{}'s decision (Table~\ref{tab:3}). \allow{} restores every correction in $H$ and nothing else, so an APPEND that lacks shared support stays held under every rule. Two predicates define the evidence checks. For \literal{} ($L_j=1$), the proposal must name one existing target; preserve its subject, relation, and time; change its value; and cite the current turn in both the fact and the operation. In addition, the replacement value must occur literally in every reading. For \witness{} ($W_j=1$), the same target and source conditions must hold, and each reading must supply exactly one witness for the target record that is labeled affirmed, repeats the proposed subject, relation, time, and value, cites the current turn, and quotes a verbatim span of that reading containing the replacement value. The application verifies the structure and linkage of these model-generated witnesses, but the polarity label itself is taken from the model.

Two examples show how the checks differ. The sentence ``the shop opened in 1993, not 1991'' satisfies both checks for an opening-year record. By contrast, ``My son works at Daehan Post'' satisfies the literal check for an update of the speaker's own workplace but should fail the witness check, which requires every reading to affirm the value for the targeted record: the affirmed statement concerns the son.

\subsection{Containment and the Opportunity to Intervene}\label{sec:bound}
The admitted sets of the four rules are nested. Write $A_X$ for the indices in $H$ that rule $X$ admits; then
\begin{equation}
A_{\rulesub{Witness}}=\{\,j\in H : W_j=1\,\}\;\subseteq\; A_{\rulesub{Literal}}=\{\,j\in H : L_j=1\,\}\;\subseteq\; A_{\rulesub{Allow}}=H .
\label{eq:containment}
\end{equation}
The first inclusion holds because a passing witness carries the required target and source linkage and an exact quotation that contains the value, so the value occurs in every reading; the second follows from the restriction to $H$. This ordering concerns admitted operations only. It need not carry over to final-state accuracy when a transaction is rejected, when operations interact, or when a witness is wrong.

\agree{} and \allow{} are therefore the two ends of this ordering rather than competing designs: no rule that acts on $H$ can admit more held corrections than \allow{} or fewer than \agree{}. We compare \literal{} and \witness{} with both ends: with \allow{} for the reference violations they prevent, and with \agree{} for the corrections they recover and the reference violations they add. Because \agree{} reads model-extracted facts, its holds can originate in extraction rather than in disagreement between readings; the saved extraction shows which of the two occurred (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}). \witness{} and \literal{} decide differently only on $K=\{\,j\in H : L_j=1,\ W_j=0\,\}$, where a hold may either protect the correct state or keep an obsolete fact unnecessarily. When $H$ is empty, all four rules must agree.

Admission and execution are deterministic given the saved case, extraction, and proposal. Replay therefore identifies effects conditional on these artifacts, rather than effects of regenerating a complete pipeline \cite{cite53}. For the four correction rules, outputs with nonempty $H$ contain every opportunity for different admission decisions. A particular rule pair may differ on only a subset; different decisions can also yield the same state or endpoint score. Exposure is counted from saved artifacts before scoring.

\begin{proposition}[Exposure bound and reference dependence]\label{prop:1}
Let rules $X$ and $Y$ act on the same endpoint population $D$, with $N_D=|D|$, and let $E_D\subseteq D$ contain every output on which the rules can make different admission decisions. For every endpoint indicator $S$ computed on the executed state, (i) $|\bar S_X-\bar S_Y|\le |E_D|/N_D$; (ii) the difference $\bar S_X-\bar S_Y$ depends on the reference only through the scores of the outputs in $E_D$; and (iii) if a second reference changes $S$ on at most $C$ of the $2|E_D|$ output--rule pairs in $E_D$, the difference changes by at most $C/N_D$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Outside $E_D$, the two rules admit the same operations, so the deterministic executor produces the same state and $S$ takes the same value under $X$ and $Y$. Hence $N_D(\bar S_X-\bar S_Y)=\sum_{o\in E_D}(S_X(o)-S_Y(o))$, whose absolute value is at most $|E_D|$. The sum involves the reference only through outputs in $E_D$, and a change to at most $C$ individual scores changes the sum by at most $C$.
\end{proof}

The exposure set and denominator must refer to the same endpoint population. Thus, 49 exposed outputs among 3,024 episodes bound an all-case endpoint difference by approximately 1.6 percentage points, not automatically a positive-change or correction-completion difference. On Gemini 2.5 Flash, 11 exposed outputs among 144 permit a 7.6-point all-case difference, whereas 11 among 33 correction observations permit a 33.3-point correction difference. For gating versus ungated execution, exposure must also cover affected appends and decisions outside $H$. Reference review must use the relevant gate-log-based set, including exposed outputs that tie under current scores (Section~\ref{sec:reference-results}).

\subsection{Execution and Complete Pipelines}\label{sec:execution}
The application applies operations transactionally to a separate SQLite state for each rule and records read-after-write execution logs; the executor enforces retention permission and target existence for every rule. Because admission does not regenerate the action or the reply, differences in state outcomes between rules can be assigned to admission alone within this pipeline. For the same reason, a held write can coexist with a reply that asserts the held value (Appendix~H), and the unchanged reply is not credited to admission. \witness{} uses the same two calls per output as \agree{}, and \allow{} and \literal{} are computed from the saved proposals (Algorithm~\ref{alg:1}; Appendix~C).

The comparison pipeline, \review{}, generates its own proposal from a draft and one general-review call, so it and the controlled path use the same number of calls. The original protocol also ran a single direct draft and one draft--feedback--refine cycle adapted from Self-Refine \cite{cite18}; they share \review{}'s initial draft where the design permits and are reported in Appendices~A and F. The internally prespecified primary test compares the controlled path, with its reply rendered from the execution log, with \review{}. Rendering does not change any state endpoint, so the tables report the common state score under \witness{} (Appendix~A).

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
\caption{Admission on a fixed proposal. Reply rendering is a separate downstream option.}\label{alg:1}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require case $x=(h,T,M,\pi)$, extraction $F$, proposal $(a,u,Q)$, rule $X$ (\agree{}, \allow{}, \literal{}, or \witness{})
\Ensure action $a$ and reply $u$ unchanged; executed state $M'$ and log $\rho$
\If{$Q$ fails schema validation}
  \State \Return $(a,u,M,\emptyset)$ \Comment{no write; scored as an invalid output}
\EndIf
\State $Q_X \gets \textsc{AgreeGate}(x,F,Q)$ \Comment{holds APPEND and CORRECT without shared support}
\ForAll{$j\in H(x,F,Q)$}
  \If{$X=\allow$ \textbf{or} ($X=\literal$ \textbf{and} $L_j=1$) \textbf{or} ($X=\witness$ \textbf{and} $W_j=1$)}
    \State $Q_{X,j}\gets Q_j$ \Comment{restore the proposed correction}
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $(M',\rho)\gets \textsc{Execute}(M,Q_X,\pi)$
\State \Return $(a,u,M',\rho)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Evaluation Design}\label{sec:design}
\subsection{Sources and Model Configurations}\label{sec:sources}
The main source contains 60 Korean test cases written by the authors with AI assistance: 12 development cases and 48 held-out cases spanning 12 problem families of four cases each (Appendix~A, Table~A3). Each held-out case was run three times per model configuration. A private reference, fixed before the main collection, specifies the requirements listed in Section~\ref{sec:task}, including the distinction between replacing a value and removing its record. The two co-first authors independently checked each of the 48 held-out references against its case; no external annotator validated them and no inter-annotator agreement statistic was computed. Held-out cases were separated from development within this study, not independently authored. The collection script received the public cases but not the reference, and the original configurations, prompts, admission rules, and evaluator were fixed before the original collection. We use Korean because widely used memory benchmarks are in English \cite{cite20,cite37} and because Korean writing yields several surface forms of one fact (word spacing varies in practice, and a person may be named by a kinship term, a given name, or both), which stresses exact tuple matching (Section~\ref{sec:reference-results}).

All alternative readings of the main source are written text authored with AI assistance. Within a case, they can be identical, differ in surface form, or differ in value, person, or polarity; they play the role of competing recognition hypotheses but were not produced by a recognizer, so how often they reach the held set characterizes this source rather than any recognizer.

The original protocol planned a Holm-adjusted primary comparison for five model families; Gemini 2.5 Flash, Llama 3.1 8B, and Qwen3 14B (local 4-bit) were collected, and two families were not (Appendix~C). Six later Gemini configurations reuse the 48 cases and were added after the earlier results had been inspected. The model panel comprises the 21 configurations that completed all 48 cases in all three repetitions with at least 95\% valid final outputs in both \review{} and the controlled path: seven Gemini, eight Claude, and three GPT configurations together with DeepSeek 3.2, GLM 5, and Qwen3 14B, for 3,024 case-repetition episodes. This validity criterion was set after collection and applies to complete pipelines, not semantic outcomes; of the 28 configurations with complete triplets, seven fall below it, among them Llama 3.1 8B (Appendix~G). Model labels denote recorded provider or runtime configurations and do not imply matched parameter counts, precision, or reasoning budgets.

Table~\ref{tab:4} lists the further sources. Three authored or adapted sources probe transfer and are reported in Appendices~D--F: a 24-case contrast set written after the initial finding by a separate AI agent that saw neither the rule code nor earlier outputs, a 12-case boundary probe, and 16 selected MultiWOZ~2.4 turns \cite{cite42}. A fourth source, 48 user turns from the DSTC2 test set, supplies real speech-recognition hypotheses with a reference derived from human-reviewed labels (Section~\ref{sec:dstc2}). Three diagnostic follow-up procedures, designed after the earlier results had been inspected and fixed before their own outputs, test the diagnoses (Appendix~J): a revised extraction instruction on the held cases, a learned verifier (Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview) that approves or holds each APPEND and CORRECT in saved \review{} proposals without seeing the extraction or the reference, and the DSTC2 replay.

\subsection{Endpoints and the Variant-Tolerant Reference}\label{sec:endpoints}
The primary endpoint internally prespecified in the original study is positive-change completion: joint success of the action and the whole state on the 28 of the 48 main cases that require a new fact or the retirement of an obsolete version. A successful output must be valid, choose an allowed action, contain the required facts, omit forbidden and obsolete tuples, preserve protected facts, satisfy the required removals, and introduce no unlisted fact; provider failures and invalid outputs stay in the denominator. Strict correction completion applies the same criterion to the 11 correction-eligible cases. The reference-violation endpoint flags forbidden or extra writes and damage to protected facts across all 48 cases. Successful positive changes and reference-violating states are always reported together, so that withholding more operations is not mistaken for improving the task.

The reference matches tuples exactly, so an unlisted fact counts as a violation even when it restates a required fact in another form. To measure how much each conclusion depends on this convention, we re-scored every saved output under a \emph{variant-tolerant reference}. A tuple counts as a required or protected fact when its relation and time match and, after Unicode NFC, case folding, and whitespace removal, its subjects are equal, both denote the participant, or one contains the other, and its values are equal or one contains the other; a participant label is never matched to another person's label. Forbidden and obsolete tuples stay exact, so the second reference can only credit outputs. It accepts 38 distinct pairs in 10 cases, all listed in Appendix~K, and the scorer reproduces every archived score of the exact reference. These are candidate equivalences, not human-validated matches: substring containment does not establish entity identity or preservation of meaning. This exploratory re-scoring demonstrates dependence on the matching convention; it does not establish that the relaxed score is more accurate or that human judgments must fall between the two scores.

\subsection{Paired Analysis}\label{sec:analysis}
For configuration $m$ and case $i$, the paired difference is $d_{mi}=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum_{r=1}^{3}\bigl[Y_{mir}(\mathrm{ctrl})-Y_{mir}(\rulesub{Review})\bigr]$, where $Y$ is the endpoint indicator for repetition $r$ and ctrl denotes the controlled path; the effect estimate averages $d_{mi}$ over cases. Uncertainty is reported as 95\% percentile intervals from 5,000 case-cluster bootstrap draws that keep the repetitions, and for pooled estimates the configurations, of a case together (48 clusters for all-case endpoints, 28 for positive completion, and 11 for correction completion). The internally prespecified exact two-sided McNemar test counts a case as a success when it succeeds in at least two of three repetitions, and the original plan adjusted the five planned family tests with Holm's method (Appendix~A). Each original-protocol run made $48\times3\times6=864$ physical calls, of which each two-call pipeline used 288, without retries. Gemini 2.5 Flash used temperature 0.2, disabled thinking, and 2,048 output tokens; later configurations used their supported thinking settings, so the panel compares configurations as run rather than at equal compute (Appendix~C). Counts pool configurations and repetitions, which are not independent replications of a case; distinct cases are reported alongside.

\subsection{Prespecified and Exploratory Analyses}\label{sec:analysis-status}
The study began with an internally prespecified comparison of the controlled path and \review{}; the protocol was fixed before the original collection but was not publicly preregistered. Replay analyses were developed after the initial results were inspected. They explain those outcomes without replacing the original test or acquiring its inferential status (Table~\ref{tab:5}). These conditions, two string-normalization controls, the variant audit, and the variant-tolerant re-scoring required no model call (Appendices~I and K).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
The original complete-pipeline comparison did not establish a completion advantage (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}). To explain the observed outcomes, we first report replay analyses that vary admission on fixed extractions and proposals, then the complete-pipeline decomposition and diagnostic follow-ups.

\subsection{Finding 1: Extraction Omissions Create Recoverable Holds}\label{sec:extraction}
On Gemini 2.5 Flash, the agreement gate withheld 11 required corrections that the other three rules admitted. Changing admission from \agree{} to \literal{} or \witness{} increases completed corrections from 22 to 33 of 33 observations, while reference-violating states remain at 3 of 144 under every rule (Table~\ref{tab:6}). The 11 recovered corrections come from five of the 11 eligible cases, and eight are repetitions of self-repair cases. The paired case-mean gain is 33.3 percentage points (95\% case-cluster interval 12.1 to 57.6), and the case-level McNemar test, with four discordant cases all favoring \witness{}, gives $p=.125$; four discordant cases are too few to resolve the gain, so we report it as a count of cases (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Because \allow{} recovers the same corrections without the evidence predicates, releasing the hold is sufficient on these proposals; this recovery does not establish an incremental benefit from evidence-based selection.

In all 11 held observations, both extracted fact lists were empty, although the correction appeared as two affirmed witnesses matching the proposal. In the other 22 observations, both fact lists contained the corrected tuple. Thus the holds arose from extraction omissions, not conflicting readings. The contrast set contains five further supported corrections with the same omission pattern, all recovered by the three releasing rules. Only one of the 17 traced holds reflects disagreement: one reading negates the new residence (Appendix~D).

A follow-up re-extracted one held repetition from each of the five cases under the original instruction and a revision that also lists affirmed corrections among the extracted facts (Appendix~J). The saved proposal and \agree{} gate were unchanged. The original instruction completed one of five corrections; the revision completed all five, with no reference violation in either arm. CT01 recovered spontaneously under the original instruction and is not an effect of the revision. On Gemini 3.8 Flash, which had zero exposure, both instructions produced identical outcomes on all 48 cases. The revised extractor was sufficient to remove the selected Flash holds. This single follow-up establishes neither transfer to new cases nor a general advantage of extraction revision over admission control.

\subsection{Finding 2: Zero Exposure Explains Many Ties}\label{sec:exposure-results}
Gemini 3 Flash Preview, 3.1 Pro Preview, and 3.8 Flash produce no output that reaches $H$ (Table~\ref{tab:6}). Their ties are instances of the opportunity confound, as is the 12-case boundary probe, in which no output reached $H$ and every rule produced the same states (Appendix~E). Across the 21 panel configurations, the saved gate decisions on controlled-path proposals show no exposed output on 12 configurations and 49 exposed outputs, from 12 cases, on the other nine (Appendix~G). Because every configuration answers the same 48 cases, the range of exposure, from 0 to 11 of 144 outputs, shows that whether this benchmark can separate admission rules depends on the generator; on general-review proposals, 16 of the 21 configurations have no exposed output (Appendix~I). Exposure therefore has to be reported per configuration, not per benchmark. By Proposition~\ref{prop:1}, no panel-wide comparison among the rules on these proposals can move an all-case endpoint by more than 49 of 3,024 outputs, or 1.6 percentage points. The same count states this benchmark's main limitation: the rules can differ on only 12 of its 48 cases (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Both evidence checks release 12 of the 49 holds, each completing a required correction (11 on Gemini 2.5 Flash and one on DeepSeek 3.2); \literal{} alone releases two more, both reference violations; and both evidence checks keep the other 35, all in cases that require no change, where the held state conforms to the reference. On most exposed outputs, the agreement gate thus withheld writes that the reference rejects, as designed.

Within the exposed outputs, a literal check supplies most of the protection that the evidence checks add. Relative to \allow{}, \witness{} avoids 14 reference-violating state observations and \literal{} 13, all on Gemini 2.5 Pro, 2.5 Flash-Lite, and 3.5 Flash-Lite and from three distinct cases, while both keep \allow{}'s strict-correction count on every configuration (Table~\ref{tab:6}). On the saved Qwen3 14B proposals, \allow{} produces 29 reference-violating states out of 144 and \literal{} and \witness{} 20 each, with strict correction at 33 of 33. Within the seven Gemini configurations, \witness{} matches \agree{}'s reference-violation count while recovering the 11 Flash corrections; the panel exposure summary also includes one recovered correction on DeepSeek 3.2.

\subsection{Target-Linked Evidence Blocked Person and Polarity Errors}\label{sec:target}
The one reference-violating state that the literal check admits on Gemini 2.5 Pro illustrates what the witness check adds (Figure~\ref{fig:5}). The utterance describes a son's workplace, and the same proposal both appends the son's workplace and corrects the participant's stored workplace. Because the replacement value occurs in both readings, \literal{}, like \allow{}, admits the second update; the witness for the participant's record is not affirmed, so \witness{} holds it and keeps the participant's workplace. Target-linked witnesses can thus block a value that appears in every reading but belongs to another person. The contrast set supplies a second situation, a value that one reading negates (Section~\ref{sec:extraction}), and the third is an uncertain report about a travel destination with a request not to change it (CT11), which \literal{} admits and \witness{} holds on GLM 5 controlled-path proposals and on saved Qwen3 14B review proposals. Literal occurrence cannot tell whose fact a value is or whether a reading affirms it; binding the evidence to the target record can, and in these four observations it changed the executed state. Section~\ref{sec:dstc2} adds a fifth, a negated value among real recognition hypotheses. The four observations outside DSTC2 represent three situations; they establish that target linkage can matter here, not its failure prevalence or a general ranking of rules.

\subsection{Finding 3: Reference Conventions Change the Gating Trade-off}\label{sec:reference-results}
Applying the gate to saved \review{} proposals more than halves reference-violating states under the exact reference, from 146 to 64 with \witness{}, at a cost of 40 positive changes (Table~\ref{tab:10}). The two bounds separate the parts of the gate: \review{}+\agree{} already reaches 64, and \review{}+\allow{} 75. Counting decisions by operation locates the difference (Appendix~I). All four rules treat APPEND identically and hold 108 of 1,087 proposed appends; these holds account for 71 of the 82 avoided reference violations and 36 of the 40 lost positive changes, and the other four losses come from invalid extractions. \agree{} holds 23 of 762 proposed corrections, on five configurations; \allow{} releases all of them, \literal{} 13, and \witness{} 12, and each release shared by these rules completes a required correction. On these proposals, the gate acts mainly as an append policy.

The avoided reference violations should not all be interpreted as independently established semantic errors. Comparing each held tuple with the states that conform to the reference for the same case, 13 of the 82 involve a forbidden tuple, and 52 of the remaining 69 are mechanically identified candidate variants of a required fact with different spacing (6), subject label (29; e.g., ``어머니 순자'' (``mother Sunja'') for ``순자''(``Sunja''), or value form (17; e.g., ``자전거 타기'' (``riding a bicycle'') for ``자전거''(``bicycle'')). Under exact matching, these 52 observations lack the required fact with or without the gate, so the hold lowers the violation count without changing completion.

The variant-tolerant reference measures how much this matters (Table~\ref{tab:10}). Under it, \review{} produces 44 reference-violating states instead of 146 and 1,725 positive changes instead of 1,623: 102 previously violating states become conforming under the mechanical matcher. The reference therefore changes the apparent balance between protection and missed changes. Neither convention has independent human validation: the exact reference lists one form per fact, while the variant-tolerant reference accepts mechanically defined equivalences (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

For the completed replay comparisons available in the archive, reported pairwise score differences among the correction rules are unchanged under the two references. Nonzero differences occur on 36 controlled-path and 23 review-proposal outputs; these score-based counts must not be substituted for the gate-log exposure counts of 49 and 23. Fourteen controlled-path \allow{} replays are missing, and an additional exposed output has tied endpoint scores (Appendices~G and K). The incomplete replays do not support an exhaustive panel-wide invariance claim. For \review{} versus \review{}+\witness{}, the reference changes the difference on 52 of the 127 outputs whose endpoints differ. Normalizing strings inside the gate changes no executed state on the main panel, whereas case differences affect the public task-dialogue probe (7 completed self-repairs under \allow{} versus 4 under the evidence checks, out of 10 eligible; Appendix~F).

\subsection{The Complete-Pipeline Difference Is Two Opposite Terms}\label{sec:pipeline}
Under the exact reference, complete-pipeline differences split evenly across configurations. Among the 21 panel configurations, the controlled path increases positive-change completion relative to general review on 9, decreases it on 9, and ties on 3, and reference violations decrease on 12, increase on 7, and tie on 2; 11 configurations change direction across repetitions (Appendix~G). The internally prespecified primary comparison on the original families did not establish a completion advantage; this does not demonstrate equivalence between pipelines. On Gemini 2.5 Flash, \review{} completes 82 of 84 positive-change observations and the controlled path 80 (difference $-2.4$ percentage points, 95\% interval $-6.0$ to $0.0$; McNemar $p=1.00$); on Qwen3 14B the difference is $-8.3$ points ($-23.8$ to $6.0$); and Llama 3.1 8B, outside the panel because its controlled path produced 68 invalid final outputs, completes 6 of 84 against 29 ($-27.4$ points, $-44.0$ to $-10.7$; Holm-adjusted $p=.0586$).

An intermediate replay condition decomposes the observed difference: \review{}+\witness{} applies the controlled path's extraction and gate to the saved review proposal. The complete-pipeline difference equals the controlled path minus this intermediate condition (proposal term), plus the intermediate condition minus \review{} (gate term; Table~\ref{tab:12}). This is a conditional decomposition through the chosen intermediate condition; it does not estimate a full factorial interaction. Under the exact reference, the controlled path's proposals add 67 reference violations and 17 positive changes relative to review proposals under the same gate (violations $+2.22$ points, interval 1.03 to 3.67), and the gate removes 82 violations and 40 positive changes; the two terms nearly cancel, leaving $-15$ and $-23$. General review also completes all 33 strict corrections on Gemini 2.5 Flash, so the fixed-proposal gain has nothing to recover there. Under the variant-tolerant reference, the proposal term is $+40$ violations and $+44$ positive changes and the gate term $-30$ and $-92$; the controlled path then completes fewer positive changes on 13 configurations and more on one (pooled $-2.72$ points, $-4.59$ to $-1.02$), and violations decrease on 6 configurations and increase on 6. The direction of the complete-pipeline comparison thus depends on the reference, and the decomposition shows why: the gate's append holds, not the extraction-informed proposals, carry the completion cost.

\subsection{Replay Locates Where a Learned Verifier Acts}\label{sec:verifier}
A learned verifier \cite{cite46} replaced the gate on one saved \review{} proposal per case for three configurations (144 proposals; Appendix~J). On Gemini 2.5 Flash it matches \review{}+\witness{} on every endpoint, including five more completed corrections than \review{}+\agree{}; these are the extraction-induced holds of Finding~1. Its other differences occur outside $H$: it admits a required append on Gemini 3.8 Flash and blocks two writes on Qwen3 14B, reducing reference violations from three to one. All paired intervals against \review{}+\witness{} include zero. These observations locate the verifier's decisions within the same underlying writer; they establish neither a ranking nor replication on an independent memory-writing architecture. The 144 calls averaged 4.39 seconds and about 850 tokens each.

\subsection{On Real Recognition Hypotheses, Replay Finds a Different Cause}\label{sec:dstc2}

The two-call pipeline, with the three slots in place of personal relations and the gate unchanged, ran once on Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, all 48 outputs valid (Table~\ref{tab:dstc2}); a partial Gemini 3.8 Flash run is reported in Appendix~J.

Exposure is high on this source: \agree{} held a proposed correction in 20 of the 48 outputs, against at most 11 of 144 for any main-source configuration. The trace also returns a different attribution. None of the 20 holds shows the pattern of Section~\ref{sec:extraction}, affirmed witnesses in every reading with the tuple missing from the fact lists; in ten, different hypotheses yield different extracted values, and in the other ten the value is supported by only some hypotheses or omitted from some. Releasing every hold (\allow{}) completes 17 required changes and introduces 3 wrong values. Neither evidence check releases any of the 17, because both require the value in every hypothesis, and \literal{} releases one hold, a wrong write. Relative to \allow{}, \witness{} lowers goal conformity by 31.25 percentage points (95\% caller-cluster interval 16.3 to 45.8) and required-change completion by 47.2 points (32.3 to 62.9), and it lowers turns with a new wrong write by 6.25 points (0.0 to 13.5).

Two turns show the trade-off. In DSTC2\_45, the caller says ``i dont want chinese i want thai food''; the stored goal is already thai, but every hypothesis contains ``chinese,'' and the proposal changes the goal to chinese. \literal{} admits the change because the value occurs in every hypothesis, whereas \witness{}, like \agree{}, holds it for lack of affirmed, target-linked witnesses: the polarity error of Section~\ref{sec:target} on a real recognizer. In DSTC2\_07, ``how about vietnamese food'' appears in some hypotheses as ``how about you need food''; \allow{} admits the correct change, and the three strict rules hold it. On this source, requiring support in every hypothesis prevents wrong writes but also withholds recoverable changes. The Korean and DSTC2 sources differ in language, domain, reference construction, number and origin of readings, and sampling, so this is not a crossed experiment that isolates recognition noise. It demonstrates the diagnosis on an additional source using the same writer architecture.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Interpreting Rule Comparisons}
The three findings call for different checks. An \allow{} comparison and the saved extraction can distinguish evidence-based selection from recovery of upstream omissions. Exposure determines whether a tie tests competing decisions. Re-scoring tests whether a reported benefit depends on the reference's representation of facts. These checks address different questions, and agreement under two mechanical references is not a substitute for human validation.

The DSTC2 result sets a boundary on the first diagnosis: there, holds involved disagreement or incomplete support across recognizer hypotheses, rather than the extraction pattern in the main source. Majority or likelihood-weighted support is a candidate intervention, with thresholds fixed on development data.

An external-writer replication should freeze artifacts at a native interface. For example, Mem0 separates extraction from ADD, UPDATE, DELETE, and NOOP decisions \cite{cite6}; a comparison could fix extracted candidates, retrieved memories, record identifiers, and the initial state while varying the update mechanism. This would test the diagnostic procedure on another architecture, but reproducing the four rules would additionally require explicit per-reading evidence and target-linked witnesses. No such replication is reported here.

\subsection{Replay Cost and Pipeline Resources}
Replaying a deterministic rule requires no new model call, but deploying the hybrid gate adds an extraction stage to the two-call \review{} pipeline. On Gemini 2.5 Flash, mean summed stage time rises from 3.00 to 4.92 seconds (64\%); this is not a serving-latency measurement. The benefit to weigh against that cost depends on the reference (Finding~3). Larger memories would also require scoping witnesses to the records targeted by a proposal.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
The study uses 48 authored Korean cases and 48 DSTC2 turns. In the main source, only 12 distinct cases reach $H$, five account for recovered corrections, and three for the literal check's protection; the witness-only holds outside DSTC2 comprise four observations from three situations. Model configurations and repetitions increase observations, not independent cases. All replays use our own extraction--proposal pipeline, and the extraction intervention was tested once on selected cases. The results establish mechanisms in these data, not their prevalence in other writers or a general ranking of admission rules. The internally prespecified primary comparison did not establish a completion advantage; under the exploratory variant-tolerant reference, the controlled path completes fewer positive changes than general review.

The authors created the main cases and references with AI assistance, and both co-first authors independently checked the held-out references. No external annotator checked them, no agreement statistic was computed, and no person validated the mechanical variant equivalences. The separate AI-generated contrast set does not provide independent human authorship or validation. Only DSTC2 supplies an externally labeled reference, while retaining our writer architecture. Witness polarity remains a model judgment despite structural and source-link checks. We do not assess semantic safety, user utility, later retrieval, or longitudinal interaction.

\section{Future Work and Conclusion}\label{sec:future work and conclusion}
The limitations above set the next priorities: independent human validation, and replication on externally authored cases and another memory writer. For the four correction rules on the main panel, review should start from the 49 controlled-path and 23 review-proposal exposed outputs, after completing the missing \allow{} replays on 14 outputs, together with the 38 candidate variant pairs. Score ties alone do not justify excluding an output; gating-versus-not-gating and learned-verifier comparisons require their own review sets. Exposure can guide additional diagnostic sampling, which must be distinguished from prevalence estimation. A prospective validation protocol should fix the review population, contextual equivalence criteria, blinding to model and rule identities, disagreement handling, and analysis before collecting judgments, and report agreement before adjudication. Any public registration would apply to that new study, not retrospectively to these results. Extraction revisions and admission thresholds should be fixed on development data before testing on independently authored new cases.

A completion score alone cannot show which stage held a memory write, whether an admission rule was ever exercised, or how much a measured gain depends on the reference. Specification-based replay separates these questions by applying each rule to the same saved proposals and scoring the outcomes against an explicit specification. In our data, it identified three mechanisms that a completion score hides. First, extraction omissions created recoverable holds: some held writes came from what extraction failed to supply, not from the admission rule's judgment, so a hold cannot be credited to admission until its source is traced. Second, zero exposure explained many rule ties: rules that never met an output on which they could differ tied by construction, so a tie means little unless exposure is reported. Third, reference conventions changed the apparent benefit of gating, so a gain measured against one reference may not hold under another. Together, these mechanisms explain why selected component gains need not translate into overall improvement. This is consistent with the internally prespecified complete-pipeline comparison, which did not establish a completion advantage.

The practical consequence is a set of reporting checks, not a preferred admission rule. Before attributing a gain to admission, an evaluation should trace each held write to the stage that produced it, report each rule's exposure alongside any comparison between rules, and rescore under alternative reference conventions. Each check targets one observed mechanism, and replay supports all three because rules and references are applied to fixed proposals. More broadly, an omitted proposal, an unexposed rule, or a shifted reference can each move an apparent gain without any change in the admission rule. Memory-write decisions should therefore be evaluated stage by stage, not by end-to-end completion alone. How often these mechanisms occur beyond our data is a question for the validation and replication outlined above, but the diagnostic questions apply to any memory writer that separates proposal from admission.
